A 100 MW battery in our region ran 312 full cycles last year. The business case
assumed 450.

The gap is not chemistry. The cells are fine. The gap is market design: the
capacity payment rewards being available, not being used.

We keep optimising the asset and ignoring the rules that decide how often it is
allowed to work.

What would change first in your market if availability stopped paying better
than delivery?

Three years into this postdoc I have learned that the hardest part of energy
research is not the experiment.

It is the eighteen months between a working prototype and anyone being allowed
to connect it to a grid.

Nobody writes a paper about those eighteen months, which is probably why so many
good results stop there.
---
Sodium-ion is not going to replace lithium.

It is going to take the applications where energy density never mattered in the
first place: stationary storage, backup power, anything that sits still.

That is still an enormous market, and it is the one worth watching. The
replacement narrative is the wrong frame, and it keeps distracting from the
question that matters, which is cost per cycle at the point of installation.
